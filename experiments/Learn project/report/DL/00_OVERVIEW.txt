================================================================================
DL PROJECT — OVERVIEW
Unsupervised anomaly detection on chest radiographs:
where do reconstruction methods actually get their accuracy from?

Dataset : RSNA Pneumonia Detection Challenge (chest X-ray), image-level
Task    : anomaly CLASSIFICATION (AnoCls) — one score per image, no labels in training
Code    : experiments/Learn project/DL/dl_project.py  (single self-contained notebook)
================================================================================

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
1. THE QUESTION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
Reconstruction-based anomaly detection trains a model on NORMAL images only, then
flags an image as abnormal when the model reconstructs it badly. Every such method
is built from two separate decisions:

    (a) the TRAINING OBJECTIVE   — what the model is optimised to reconstruct
    (b) the ANOMALY SCORE        — how the residual is turned into one number

In the literature these two are almost always changed TOGETHER and reported under a
single name. AE-SSIM (Bergmann et al., 2019) trains on 1-SSIM and scores with 1-SSIM.
AE-PL (Shvetsova et al., 2021) trains and scores with a VGG feature distance. AE-U
(Mao et al., 2020) trains with an uncertainty-weighted loss and scores with the same
uncertainty weighting. So when AE-SSIM beats a plain AE, the published number cannot
say whether the gain came from (a), from (b), or from their interaction.

    THIS PROJECT SEPARATES (a) FROM (b) AND MEASURES EACH.

The exception in the literature is the gradient-score family (Zimmerer et al., 2019),
which does change only the score and keeps the objective fixed. That prior work is
what shows the question is worth asking; nobody has asked it for the reconstruction
metrics themselves.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
2. WHAT WAS DONE, IN ORDER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
STEP 1 — Reproduce the baselines faithfully.
    Ten published methods re-implemented from their original papers and trained under
    one identical protocol (same split, same architecture, same optimiser, 3 seeds).
    Purpose: nothing built on top means anything if the baselines are wrong.
    -> see 03_RESULTS_BASELINES.txt

STEP 2 — A1: cross the training objective with the anomaly score.
    Train an autoencoder with each of {L2, SSIM, perceptual, uncertainty}; score every
    trained model with each of {L2, SSIM, perceptual, gradient, uncertainty}. The
    diagonal reproduces the published methods; the off-diagonal is new.
    -> see 04_EXP_A1_SCORE_VS_OBJECTIVE.txt

STEP 3 — A3: if different scores read different things, combine them.
    Rank-average the score vectors already produced in Step 1. No training at all.
    Two combinations pre-registered before looking at any result.
    -> see 05_EXP_A3_ENSEMBLE.txt

STEP 4 — A4: if a method's gain is really in its score, get that score without its training.
    Freeze a trained plain autoencoder, fit ONLY a variance head on top of it, and
    score as AE-U does. Sweep the head's capacity so a failure cannot be blamed on size.
    -> see 06_EXP_A4_POSTHOC_UNCERTAINTY.txt

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
3. HEADLINE RESULTS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
R1. THE BASELINES REPRODUCE.
    9 of 10 methods land within one standard deviation of their published values;
    four within 0.2 AUC points. The one exception is DAE (n=1 seed only).

R2. THE ANOMALY SCORE CARRIES MOST OF THE REPORTED GAIN.
    Across the A1 grid, varying the SCORE moves accuracy by 26.7 AUC points; varying
    the TRAINING OBJECTIVE moves it by 14.2. Concretely, a plain L2 autoencoder simply
    RESCORED with SSIM reaches 77.4 vs its own 68.3 — 73% of AE-SSIM's entire published
    advantage, with no retraining whatsoever.

R3. BUT THE OBJECTIVE AND THE SCORE MUST MATCH.
    Training on a metric and scoring with a different one is actively harmful, not
    merely suboptimal. Every "trained on X, scored with L2" cell falls BELOW the plain
    autoencoder, and the perceptually-trained model scored in pixel space drops to 44.9
    — below chance. The objective's role is not to build a better reconstruction; it is
    to shape WHAT KIND OF RESIDUAL is left behind so the matching metric can read it.

R4. AE-U's ADVANTAGE IS ALMOST ENTIRELY ITS SCORING RULE.
    AE-U reaches 86.9 vs the plain AE's 68.3. But AE-U scored with plain L2 gives 65.4
    — WORSE than the plain autoencoder. Uncertainty training does not produce a better
    reconstructor. Confirmed constructively in A4: a variance head fitted post hoc onto
    a FROZEN plain autoencoder recovers 74% of that advantage for 0.62% extra parameters.
    The remaining 26% (4.81 points) is what joint training genuinely buys.

R5. COMBINING TWO COMPLEMENTARY SCORES BEATS THE BEST PUBLISHED METHOD.
    A rank-average of the perceptual and uncertainty scores reaches 88.74 vs the best
    single method's 87.58, with zero additional training. It survives a selection-aware
    validation: chosen on one half of the test set, it wins on the unseen half in 100%
    of 150 splits.
    Reported alongside its own negative control: the two PRE-REGISTERED ensembles both
    LOST, because equal-weight averaging is not robust to a weak member.

A NOTE ON ATTRIBUTION
    Methods are described from the papers that INTRODUCED them, not from the benchmark
    that later compared them. Cai et al. (2025) is used for one purpose only: as the
    reference column our reproduction is checked against. Where this project follows
    their implementation (architecture, hyperparameters, data split) that is stated
    explicitly, because it is what makes the comparison fair.
